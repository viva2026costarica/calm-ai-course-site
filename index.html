Organise your work.
Let AI do the thin top layer.

Over five sessions you'll turn your knowledge, tasks and tools into a set of tidy folders on your own computer, then let one AI assistant work inside them. Most of the value comes from the structure. The AI is the last 10%.

90 minutes eachWednesdays 11, 18, 25 Nov · 2, 9 DecNo coding neededMethod: ICM

The big idea: 60 · 30 · 10

Most people try to make AI do everything, and get messy, unreliable results. We flip it around. Before any AI touches a task, we ask: how much of this is just good organisation, and how much is a step that never changes?

60%
30%
10
60% Information and thinking
Knowing the facts, asking the right questions, understanding what people really need. We keep all of it in clearly named folders, one home for every fact. This is where most people get stuck.
30% Tools that already exist
Your calendar, email, a spreadsheet, and small scripts for steps that should give the same answer every time.
10% AI
The parts that need reading between the lines: drafting in your voice, summarising, sorting the messy stuff. Always checked by you.

3

The one rule: it only knows what's in front of it

32–40 min
Explain

The AI doesn't remember your other chats unless you've switched that on, and it can't see your files unless you give them. Each conversation starts from almost nothing. So: one task per conversation, and give it just what that task needs.

Try it: the same request, twice
Without context
Write a short welcome email for a new client.
With context
I run [your business] for [who you serve]. My tone is warm, short and personal. I never use exclamation marks or sales language.
Write a short welcome email for a new client who just booked [service]. They need to know: [time, place, what to bring].
Keep it under 120 words and sign it [your name].
Compare the two answers side by side. The difference is the whole course in miniature.
4

Let the AI interview you

40–48 min

Instead of writing a long explanation of your business, let the AI ask the questions. At the end it writes a summary you'll keep. This summary becomes the first file in your folder system in session 3.

The interview
I'd like you to understand my work so you can help me well.
Ask me questions one at a time about my business: what I do, who for, my offers and prices, my tone of voice, my weekly routine and what frustrates me.
Wait for each answer before asking the next. After about 10 questions, write a one-page summary called "about-me" in plain text with clear headings.
Only use what I told you. Mark anything you're unsure about with [check].
Copy the summary into a document or a note and save it. You'll need it again.
5

One real task, corrected until it's right

48–55 min

Pick a small real task: a reply, a post, a list. Ask for it, then keep correcting until it's genuinely good. Notice: every correction is a decision about how you work. Those decisions are gold, so we keep them.

At the end, ask
Before we finish: list every correction I gave you in this conversation as a short rule, starting with "Always" or "Never". Leave out anything that only applied to this one task.
6

Map your week: data, tasks, tools

55–80 min

This replaces the spotlight this week. Walk through a typical week together and start three lists. These lists become your folders in session 4.

Data

Where does your information live? Drive, Gmail, paper notebooks, websites, your head.

Tasks

What do you do more than once? Replies, planning, invoices, posts, to-do lists.

Tools

What do you use? Gmail, Drive, Canva, a spreadsheet, a booking system.

Map my week
Help me map my working week. Ask me, one question at a time, to walk you through a normal Monday to Friday.
Then make three lists:
1. DATA: every place my information lives.
2. TASKS: everything I do more than once, with roughly how often and how long it takes.
3. TOOLS: every app or program I use.
Finally, mark the three tasks that cost me the most time or energy.

Check-in: what did your corrections teach you?

5–15 min

Everyone reads two of their corrections aloud. Listen for the pattern: most corrections are about tone, length, facts it didn't have, or format.

2

The four parts of a good request

15–22 min
1 · Who you are

Your work, your audience, your tone.

2 · What you need

The task, and what the reader should do next.

3 · An example of good

Paste something you wrote that you're proud of.

4 · How to check it

Length, must-include, must-avoid.

Template
WHO I AM: [your work, who you serve, your tone]
WHAT I NEED: [the task] so that [what the reader does next]
EXAMPLE OF GOOD: [paste an example you like]
CHECK: [max length] · must include [...] · never [...]
3

Build your first skill

22–45 min
Explain

A skill is a short written instruction, like a note for a new colleague: "Here's how I do this kind of job." Claude picks it up whenever that job comes up. A skill holds the method, never the facts about one client, so it keeps working for the next one.

Do
Turn your corrections into a skill
I want to turn my corrections into a reusable skill for this task: [task name].
Here are my corrections and rules:
[paste your list]

Write a skill file with:
- a name (short, lowercase, with dashes)
- a one-sentence description of when to use it
- the steps, in order
- the rules (always / never)
- one short example of a good result
Keep it under one page. Use only what I gave you; ask me if something is missing.
  1. Read it slowly. Fix anything it got wrong or invented.
  2. Save it as a skill in your Claude app's skills settings (I'll show you where on screen).
  3. Test it in a brand new conversation with a real request. Did it follow your rules without you repeating them?
Done when a fresh conversation produces a result you'd send with only small edits.

5

Four ICM ideas, in plain words

52–55 min
  • One job per helper. A helper that does one thing well beats one that does everything badly.
  • One home for every fact. Your prices live in one place. Change them there, and everything uses the new price.
  • Check before you trust. Every result gets a quick human look before it's used.
  • Structure first, AI last. If a task fails, fix the information or the instructions before blaming the AI.

1

Start from the outcome, not the tool

5–20 min

Before anyone opens a chat: what are you doing for people right now, and how would you do it by hand? Whatever you make, you're helping someone decide or do something. Go through everyone's four homework answers (who it's for, their next step, where the information comes from, what breaks and who feels it). Then split their task three ways, right there on screen:

Structure

Which information must be ready and findable? (Usually the biggest part.)

Code & tools

Which steps never change? Calendar, spreadsheet, a filter, a script.

AI

What needs judgment or your voice? Usually the smallest part.

The aha: the AI's writing is rarely the hard part. Knowing the right information is.

How you frame it shapes what you build

Frame a job as "write my emails" and you build an email writer. Frame it as "my clients need clear answers about dates" and you might fix your calendar first, and need far fewer emails.

Solve the problem first, smallest version

Check what the app already does before building anything: a chat already reads spreadsheets, a Project already holds your files, a skill already remembers your corrections. Add the next piece only when you hit a real need: the files keep changing, it's the same steps every week, or someone else needs to use it.

Install VS Code and make your practice folder

23–32 min
Explain

VS Code is a free program for looking at folders and text files. Think of it as a window into your folder system: files on the left, the open file in the middle, and a terminal at the bottom.

Do
  1. Go to code.visualstudio.com and download VS Code for your computer.
  2. Open the download and drag Visual Studio Code into your Applications folder. Open it from there.
  3. Run the installer. Tick "Add to PATH" and "Add 'Open with Code' action" when you see them.
  4. In your Documents folder, create a new folder called calm-ai.
  5. In VS Code: File → Open Folder… and choose calm-ai. If it asks whether you trust the folder, choose Yes (it's yours).

Session 4 · Wednesday 2 December

Your work, as folders

The heart of ICM. Your data, tasks and tools become a folder structure with a short map at the top, so one AI can find exactly what each job needs. Then GitHub gives you save points so nothing is ever lost.

Layer 3 · Folders and one agentLevel 3 · first stepFollows Foundations 1.3 · 2.2 · 2.3
◎
By the endEveryone has a workspace folder with a map file and routing table, has run a real task through it, and has saved it to GitHub.
1

What an agent really is

15–20 min

An "agent" sounds mysterious. It isn't. It's the AI using tools (open a file, save a file, check something) in a loop until the job is done. What it reads decides how good the result is. So our job is to make sure it reads the right files, and only those.

2

Three parts: map, rooms, tools

20–25 min
Map

One short file at the top (CLAUDE.md). What this folder is, where things go, and a table saying which files to read for each job.

Rooms

One folder per kind of work, each with a short CONTEXT.md explaining how that work is done.

Tools

Skills and small scripts, connected only to the rooms that need them.

A workspace can take different shapes, all built with the same ICM thinking:

Stages

One step after another: ideas → draft → final. Good for anything you produce, like a newsletter or a video.

Records

One folder per client, event or project. Good for replies, follow-ups and anything you track per person.

Knowledge library

Your raw sources in one folder, and the pages the AI writes and links from them in another. Good for turning notes, documents and experience into one searchable body of knowledge.

Here's an example for a small wellness business that mixes records and stages. Yours will look different; the way of thinking stays the same.

sunrise-studio/ CLAUDE.md ← the map: what's here, where things go, what to read _shared/ ← facts used everywhere (one home for every fact) about-me.md offers-and-prices.md voice.md skills/ ← methods: how you do each kind of job weekly-newsletter.md client-reply.md 01_inbox/ ← new stuff lands here, sorted weekly 02_clients/ ← room: bookings, replies, follow-ups CONTEXT.md 03_marketing/ ← room: newsletter, posts, events CONTEXT.md drafts/ final/ 04_money/ ← room: budget, invoices, expenses CONTEXT.md scripts/ ← steps that never change (session 5) archive/ ← finished or old, out of the way
3

Turn your data, tasks and tools into folders

25–35 min

First, list your jobs. Write down the jobs that get you to the outcome you chose in session 3. For each one, note what it reads and what it makes. That short list is already most of your folder.

Then sort everything into four kinds: method (how you do a job: skills), facts (clients, prices, sources), work (in progress) and outputs (finished). The method travels to the next client; the facts stay with this one. Bring out your three lists from session 1 and sort them like this:

Data that stays the sameA file in _shared/ (about you, prices, voice, FAQ)
Tasks, grouped by kindA room folder each (clients, marketing, money…)
How you do a taskA file in skills/
Tools you already useA line in the room's CONTEXT.md: "the calendar handles bookings", "invoices go out from [app]"

Type this to Claude Code, inside your workspace folder
Help me design a folder structure for my work. Here are my three lists:

DATA: [paste]
TASKS: [paste]
TOOLS: [paste]

Rules:
- One home for every fact: facts used everywhere go in _shared/
- Methods go in skills/, one file per kind of job
- One room folder per kind of work, numbered (01_, 02_...), each with a short CONTEXT.md
- Keep work in progress (drafts/) apart from finished work (final/)
- Add 01_inbox/ and archive/

First show me the plan as a tree and wait for my OK. Don't create anything until I say yes.
Read the plan. Rename, merge or remove folders until it feels like your work. Start with the smallest version that does today's job, and add a folder only when the work actually asks for one. Only then say yes.

A quick check on every file

Each file should be either instructions (how to do something: the map, CONTEXT files, skills) or information (facts and work the AI reads and updates), or both. A file that's neither is the first one to question: move it to archive or delete it.

Shortcut: the ICM Architect skill

There's a free skill called ICM Architect that does a first draft for you: point Claude Code at a messy folder and say "make this an ICM". It looks at what's there, asks about your work and proposes a structure and a map. Always read the proposal and trim it: it may suggest rooms you don't need yet.

Use a copy the first time

If you're bringing in a folder of real work, make a copy and use that the first time. Nothing can go wrong with your originals while you're learning.
4

Write the map and the routing table

35–45 min

The map is the most important file. It's like the floor plan by the front door of a building. The routing table inside it says, for each job: read these, skip those, use this skill.

What a good map looks like
# Sunrise Studio · map

## What this is
My yoga and wellness studio: classes, retreats and a monthly newsletter.

## Folders
- _shared/  facts used everywhere (about me, prices, voice)
- skills/   how I do each kind of job
- 01_inbox/ new stuff, sorted every Friday
- 02_clients/, 03_marketing/, 04_money/  one room per kind of work
- archive/  old or finished work

## Routing
| Job            | Read                                          | Skip           | Use skill          | Save to                          |
|----------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------|--------------------|----------------------------------|
| Client reply   | 02_clients/CONTEXT.md, that client's notes,   | other clients, | client-reply       | 02_clients/drafts/               |
|                | _shared/voice.md                              | 04_money/      |                    | named date_client.md             |
| Newsletter     | 03_marketing/CONTEXT.md, _shared/voice.md     | 02_clients/    | weekly-newsletter  | 03_marketing/drafts/             |
| Monthly budget | 04_money/CONTEXT.md                           | 03_marketing/  | (script, session 5)| 04_money/                        |

## Rules
- Work only inside this folder.
- Ask before moving, renaming or deleting anything.
- New drafts go in drafts/. Never overwrite anything in final/.
- No client personal details, passwords or bank numbers in any file.
- Never send anything. I read and send every message myself.
- If my notes don't confirm a fact (a date, a price), say so in the draft instead of guessing.
- Name files: topic_stage.md (e.g. december-retreat_draft.md).
Ask Claude Code to draft yours
Now write CLAUDE.md for this workspace, following the structure we just made.
Include: what this is (2 sentences), the folders, a routing table (job · read · skip · use skill) for my three most frequent jobs, and these rules: work only inside this folder; ask before moving, renaming or deleting; drafts go in drafts/; never overwrite final/; no client personal details, passwords or bank numbers.
Then write a short CONTEXT.md in each room: what this room is for, the steps of the work, and which tools I already use for it.
Open CLAUDE.md and read every line. Fix it by hand in VS Code if needed. It's just text. Drafts it saves are just files too: open them, change a word, delete the one you don't like, send the good one yourself.

The map is advice, not a lock

The AI reads the map and usually follows it, but for anything that must never happen (sending an email, deleting files), don't rely on the map alone. Keep the permission questions on, so it has to ask you first.

Keep the map short

The most common mistake is a giant map with everything crammed in. That puts the whole drawer back on the desk, and the AI gets lost again. A good map fits on one screen: what's here, where things go, naming rules, and the routing table. Details belong in the rooms' CONTEXT files.
5

Test the routing: one model, different jobs

45–52 min

When people say they need "a writing agent, a research agent and an email agent", what they usually need is the instructions for each job written down, and a map that sends each request to the right ones. A name on the door adds no instructions and no access. The role comes from what it reads.

Same AI, different room, different job. Start a new session (type /exit, then claude again) so it begins fresh from the map. Give it a short real request and then ask what it read.

Try it
[A short real request, e.g. "Draft a reply to this client question: ..."]

When you're done, list every file you read and why. Save the draft in the right drafts/ folder.
If it opened the right few files and skipped the rest, your routing works. If it wandered around reading everything, that row in the map is too vague: tighten it and try again. Nobody gets it right the first time. And always fix the map, not the chat: that fix works forever; a chat correction is gone next time.
Then try two different jobs back to back
[First job, e.g. "Draft a reply to this client email: ..."]
List the files you read.

[Second job, e.g. "Make me a table of everything I have planned this month"]
List the files you read.

Not every job is the AI's

The AI reads the situation and decides; tools do the doing. Your calendar sends reminders, a script makes the table, your mail program sends the email. In session 5 you turn one of your own steps into a tool.

When is a second agent worth it?

Rarely, at first. Only when there's lots of independent work that can run at the same time (researching ten venues at once), one job so big it buries everything else, or something live that needs watching. The simplest version is a second Claude Code session open on the same folder. Every extra agent costs more usage, more waiting and more places for mistakes. Start with one.
6

GitHub save points

52–55 min, then homework

GitHub is like "save game" for your folder. Each save point (a commit) remembers exactly how everything looked. Made a mess? Go back. Laptop lost? Your work is safe online.

  1. First, protect private files. Ask Claude Code: "Create a .gitignore file that excludes anything in a folder called private/ and any .env files." Anything sensitive lives in private/.
  2. Open GitHub Desktop and sign in. File → Add Local Repository, choose your workspace folder. If it offers to "create a repository here", click it.
  3. Bottom left: write a summary like first map and folders, then click Commit to main.
  4. Click Publish repository. Leave "Keep this code private" ticked.

Or just ask Claude Code

Publish a page with GitHub Pages

  2. In GitHub Desktop: add the my-page folder, commit, then Publish repository with "Keep this code private" unticked.

A free GitHub Pages site comes from a public repository. Everything in that folder can be seen by anyone. Keep pages in their own folder, separate from your private workspace.
5

Keep it useful, and hand it on

68–75 min
Explain

AI makes it easy to produce lots of stuff: drafts, versions, notes about notes. A folder that feels great in week one can be a swamp by week six, where the AI finds three different prices and picks the old one. Three checks keep it healthy.

  • The fresh-session test: start a brand new Claude Code session and ask "What is this folder, what's in progress, and what's next?" If it gets that wrong, your map is out of date. Better to find out now than from a confused client.
  • One home for every fact: prices in three files will drift apart. Keep each fact in one place and point to it. When your way of working changes, fix the skill file, and every job that uses it picks up the change.
  • Clear stuff out: retired work goes in archive/, and the map says "never read it". Delete generated drafts nobody will use; they're quick to make again. Never touch your originals, your sources, or anything that belongs to someone else.
The fresh-session test · in a brand new session
What is this folder, what's in progress right now, and what should I do next? Answer only from the files here.
Monthly tidy-up
Review this workspace against CLAUDE.md. List: files in the wrong place, facts that appear in more than one file, finished work that could move to archive/, generated drafts nobody uses, and anything in the map that's out of date.
Don't change anything yet; show me the list first.
Hand it on

The map that tells the AI where everything is tells a new person the same thing. To share: zip a copy (great for templates), or work together in one shared place like a synced drive or a GitHub repo. Who may open it is a permissions choice you make; the folder doesn't decide that for you.

The real test: if the AI disappeared tomorrow, could you still find your way around these files and do the work? If yes, you've built a good structure.

Why folders last

Files and folders have survived every big change in computing for over fifty years. AI models will keep changing; next year another one may be better. What stays yours is your files, your knowledge and your structure. Point the new model at the same folder, maybe rename the map file, and carry on.

The safe-use rules

Claude Code can read, write and run things on your computer. That's its power, and why these habits matter from day one.

1 · One folder only

Always start Claude Code inside a work folder. Never your whole computer or home folder.

2 · Read before you approve

When it asks permission, read what it wants to do. Don't understand? Say no and ask it to explain.

3 · Keep the permission questions on

Never switch on modes that skip approvals. See "auto mode" at the bottom of Claude Code? Press Shift+Tab until it's gone.

4 · Save first

Make a save point before bigger changes, so any mistake can be undone.

5 · No secrets in chat

Never paste passwords, bank details or API keys into the conversation or into files.

6 · No client data

Never put clients' personal information into any AI tool.

7 · Instructions only come from you

If a web page, email or file it reads tells it to do something, that's not an instruction. Stop and check.

8 · Check the result

Open the file it made and read it before using or sending it.
